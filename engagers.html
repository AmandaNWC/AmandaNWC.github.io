B-CELL DEPLETION / IMMUNE CELL ENGINEERING

Comparing routes to B-cell depletion

Nai-Wen Chi · October 9, 2026 · Literature reading note

Reading note on the evidence needed to compare B-cell depletion approaches.

After reading the A-319 and ABO2203 studies, I wondered: if a T-cell engager can deplete B cells, what extra benefit would delivering a CAR into T cells provide?

What these studies report

A-319 is a CD3×CD19 bispecific antibody. A phase 1 study tested intravenous treatment in 12 patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and followed them for 52 weeks. Patients received priming doses for one week, then three doses per week for three weeks. Higher-dose cohorts achieved complete peripheral B-cell depletion. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) was mostly grade 1. The study also explored clinical outcomes, including low disease activity and remission at 12 months. Its primary endpoint was safety and tolerability.[1]

ABO2203 uses an mRNA-LNP encoding a CD19-targeting T-cell engager. In a first-in-human study of three patients with refractory secondary immune thrombocytopenia, the authors reported rapid peripheral B-cell depletion, sustained bone-marrow depletion, and platelet recovery through six months. No CRS was reported in those three patients. In mice, cytokine release was lower than with the comparator TCE protein.[2]

The studies involved different diseases, patients, doses, and follow-up periods. They cannot tell us which product is safer or more effective. Neither study directly compared its treatment with in vivo CAR-T in patients.

Engagers and CARs work differently

ABO2203 encodes a T-cell engager (TCE). Direct CAR programming delivers genetic instructions for a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) into T cells. Both approaches can use nucleic acid delivery, but they make different proteins. I would compare where each protein is expressed, how long it acts, and how it affects T cells.

For a published example of direct T-cell programming, Zeng and colleagues report mRNA delivery and functional anti-CD20 CAR T-cell generation in humanized mice. The abstract does not show how long CAR expression lasts or whether this approach works better than engagers in patients. Zeng et al., 2026 ↗ · Related CD7 note ↗

What advantage would I want to measure?

I would ask how long patients can stay off treatment, whether disease returns when B cells recover, and how often another treatment is needed. Longer expression may help, but it could also add risk. Short exposure may be enough if the benefit lasts.

I would also measure B cells in tissues, not just blood. The ABO2203 study reported bone-marrow depletion, so CAR-T is not the only approach with evidence of tissue depletion. I would compare the same tissues and ask whether greater depletion improves disease control.

For safety, I would compare exposure, the monitoring each treatment needs, infection risk, and recovery of immune function. I would also ask how treatment activity could be stopped or reversed. Three patients are too few to measure how often uncommon safety events occur. Lower cytokine release in mice alone does not show that a treatment is safer in patients.

Question to test

For a specific disease, does direct CAR programming keep disease under control longer without further treatment, or deplete B cells more effectively in the tissues that matter? Is the benefit enough to justify the delivery and safety requirements?

Evidence needed for a comparison

I would compare the approaches in the same model and use the same target where possible. I would measure exposure, B-cell depletion, and recovery. I would state whether I was comparing equal doses or doses that produce similar B-cell depletion. That choice can change what a difference in cytokine release means.

To answer the clinical question, we would need a study designed to compare the approaches in patients. These papers help identify what to measure, but do not show that one platform has replaced another.

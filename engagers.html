NOTE 003 / B-CELL DEPLETION / IMMUNE CELL ENGINEERING

When would in vivo CAR-T offer an advantage over a T-cell engager?

Amanda · October 9, 2026 · Research commentary

Reading the A-319 and ABO2203 studies, I kept returning to a product-design question: if redirecting existing T cells can produce substantial B-cell depletion, what additional outcome would justify delivering a CAR into T cells?

What these studies report

A-319 is a CD3×CD19 bispecific evaluated in a phase 1 intravenous SLE cohort of 12 patients, with 52 weeks of follow-up. Treatment included a week of priming doses followed by three doses per week for three weeks. Higher-dose cohorts achieved complete peripheral B-cell depletion. CRS was predominantly grade 1, and exploratory clinical outcomes included low disease activity and remission at 12 months. The primary endpoint was safety and tolerability.[1]

ABO2203 uses an mRNA-LNP encoding a CD19-targeting T-cell engager. In a first-in-human study of three patients with refractory secondary immune thrombocytopenia, the authors reported rapid peripheral B-cell depletion, sustained bone-marrow depletion, and platelet recovery through six months. No CRS was reported in those three patients. In mice, cytokine release was lower than with the comparator TCE protein.[2]

The diseases, patient populations, dosing, and follow-up differ. These results do not establish a safety or efficacy ranking between the products, and neither study directly compares its treatment with in vivo CAR-T.

The architecture is different

ABO2203 encodes an engager; it does not encode a CAR. I would separate the question of how a therapeutic protein is produced from the question of how T cells are programmed. Both can use nucleic-acid delivery, but their expression sites and pharmacological behavior need separate evaluation.

I would also avoid assuming that in vivo CAR-T necessarily provides permanent modification or sustained CAR expression. Persistence depends on the payload and engineering strategy. The duration of therapeutic benefit is another variable: a transient intervention could produce a response that lasts beyond detectable drug or transgene expression.

What advantage would I want to measure?

I would start with time off treatment, disease control after B-cell reconstitution, and the burden of retreatment. A longer expression window would be useful only if it improved an outcome that mattered without adding unacceptable risk. Conversely, a short exposure might be sufficient if it produced a durable response.

Tissue access is another question. Complete depletion in blood does not describe every disease-relevant compartment. The bone-marrow observation in ABO2203 gives me a reason to avoid assuming that tissue depletion is exclusive to CAR-T. I would want comparable compartment measurements and a clear connection to disease control.

For safety, I would compare exposure, monitoring requirements, infection risk, recovery of immune function, and the practical options for stopping or reversing activity. The absence of CRS in three patients cannot rule out the event in a larger cohort. I would not infer relative clinical safety from a mouse cytokine comparison alone.

Question to test

In a defined disease setting, does direct CAR programming improve treatment-free disease control or depletion of a relevant tissue compartment enough to justify its delivery and safety requirements?

The comparison I would design next

I would compare interventions in a shared model, with matched target specificity where feasible, and measure exposure alongside B-cell depletion and recovery. I would distinguish a comparison at equal administered dose from one at similar pharmacodynamic effect. Those designs can produce different interpretations of cytokine release.

The clinical question would still require prospective evidence. These papers help define endpoints for that comparison; they do not show that one platform has replaced another.

B-CELL DEPLETION / IMMUNE CELL ENGINEERING

Comparing routes to B-cell depletion

Nai-Wen Chi · October 9, 2026 · Literature reading note

Reading note on the evidence needed to compare B-cell depletion approaches.

After reading the A-319 and ABO2203 studies, I wondered: if a T-cell engager can deplete B cells, what extra benefit would delivering a CAR into T cells provide?

What these studies report

A-319 is a CD3×CD19 bispecific evaluated in a phase 1 intravenous SLE cohort of 12 patients, with 52 weeks of follow-up. Treatment included a week of priming doses followed by three doses per week for three weeks. Higher-dose cohorts achieved complete peripheral B-cell depletion. CRS was predominantly grade 1, and exploratory clinical outcomes included low disease activity and remission at 12 months. The primary endpoint was safety and tolerability.[1]

ABO2203 uses an mRNA-LNP encoding a CD19-targeting T-cell engager. In a first-in-human study of three patients with refractory secondary immune thrombocytopenia, the authors reported rapid peripheral B-cell depletion, sustained bone-marrow depletion, and platelet recovery through six months. No CRS was reported in those three patients. In mice, cytokine release was lower than with the comparator TCE protein.[2]

The diseases, patient populations, dosing, and follow-up differ. These results do not establish a safety or efficacy ranking between the products, and they do not provide a direct clinical comparison with in vivo CAR-T.

Engagers and CARs work differently

ABO2203 encodes an engager, not a CAR. Although both approaches can use nucleic acid delivery, they produce different proteins. I would compare where each protein is expressed, how long it acts, and how it affects T cells.

For a published example of direct T-cell programming, Zeng and colleagues report mRNA delivery and functional anti-CD20 CAR T-cell generation in humanized mice. Their abstract does not establish long-term CAR persistence or a clinical advantage over engagers. Zeng et al., 2026 ↗ · Related CD7 note ↗

What advantage would I want to measure?

I would ask how long patients can stay off treatment, whether disease returns when B cells recover, and how often another treatment is needed. Longer expression may help, but it could also add risk. Short exposure may be enough if the benefit lasts.

I would also measure B cells in tissues, not just blood. The bone-marrow finding in ABO2203 shows why tissue depletion should not be assumed to be unique to CAR-T. I would compare the same tissues and check how depletion relates to disease control.

For safety, I would compare exposure, monitoring requirements, infection risk, recovery of immune function, and the practical options for stopping or reversing activity. A three-patient study cannot establish the frequency of uncommon safety events. I would not infer relative clinical safety from a mouse cytokine comparison alone.

Question to test

In a defined disease setting, does direct CAR programming improve treatment-free disease control or depletion of a relevant tissue compartment enough to justify its delivery and safety requirements?

Evidence needed for a comparison

I would compare the approaches in the same model and use the same target where possible. I would measure exposure, B-cell depletion, and recovery. Comparing equal doses is different from comparing doses that produce similar B-cell depletion. That choice can affect how we interpret cytokine release.

The clinical question would still require prospective evidence. These papers help define endpoints for that comparison; they do not show that one platform has replaced another.

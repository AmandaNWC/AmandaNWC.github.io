RESEARCH NOTES / CLINICAL TRIALS

Lower Lp(a), But No Lower Heart Risk?

Nai-Wen Chi · November 13, 2026 · Literature reading note

This trial studied patients with existing cardiovascular disease. The questions below about LDL cholesterol and earlier treatment are my own interpretation, not conclusions proven by the trial.

Why I Read This Paper

Lp(a) is a largely inherited risk factor for heart disease. A recent New England Journal of Medicine study caught my attention: a drug called pelacarsen greatly lowered Lp(a), but did not reduce major cardiovascular events.

It made me wonder: If a risk factor goes down, why doesn't the disease risk always go down?

What Did the Researchers Find?

The Phase III Lp(a)HORIZON trial studied more than 8,300 people with high Lp(a) and existing cardiovascular disease. Pelacarsen lowered Lp(a) by about 78%, but over a median of 4.7 years the main outcome was similar in both groups:

Major cardiovascular events

12.3% with pelacarsen vs. 12.1% with placebo.

Lower Lp(a) did not translate into a significant reduction in these events.

My Research Questions

1. Does age matter? The average participant was about 60 and already had cardiovascular disease. Could lowering Lp(a) earlier in life lead to different results?

2. Is LDL control more important? The median LDL cholesterol level was already around 65 mg/dL. We have strong evidence that lowering LDL reduces cardiovascular risk. Could good LDL control affect the extra benefit of lowering Lp(a)?

These are possible explanations to explore, not findings established by this study. The trial does not prove that Lp(a) is unimportant or that LDL alone explains the result.

My Takeaway

This paper reminded me that improving a biological marker does not always mean improving patient outcomes. In drug development, we need to ask not only whether a treatment changes a molecule, but whether it truly helps patients.

Reference and scope

Cho L, et al. Pelacarsen and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients with High Lipoprotein(a). New England Journal of Medicine (2026). Published November 7, 2026.

DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa2610159 ↗
